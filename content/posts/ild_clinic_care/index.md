## Why This Matters

Subspecialty interstitial lung disease (ILD) clinics concentrate the multidisciplinary expertise needed to diagnose and manage a complex group of diseases. But access to that expertise is uneven, even inside tertiary health systems. Patients with ILD are managed across many ambulatory settings, and the consequences of those different care pathways are not always visible when studies focus only on specialty-clinic populations.

Our central question was whether patient characteristics, care delivery, and outcomes differed between patients managed in a subspecialty ILD clinic and those managed elsewhere within the same academic health system.

## The Study in a Nutshell

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of **6,637 adults with ILD** at a large academic medical center.

* **Subspecialty cohort:** 4,371 patients (66%) were seen in the ILD clinic.
* **Non-specialty cohort:** 2,266 patients (34%) were managed in other ambulatory settings.
* **Data:** We extracted demographic, diagnostic testing, treatment, healthcare utilization, and survival information from the electronic health record.
* **Analysis:** Multivariable logistic regression was used for diagnostic and treatment measures. Hospitalization, emergency department visits, and death were analyzed as time-to-event outcomes.

## Who Was Reached by Each Care Pathway?

Patients managed outside the subspecialty clinic were more likely to identify as Black or Latinx, live in more socioeconomically deprived neighborhoods, and have an unspecified ILD diagnosis (p < 0.001 for each comparison).

This means the differences were not limited to clinical processes. The care pathways also reached different patient populations, raising the possibility that unequal access to subspecialty expertise contributes to inequities within a single health system.

## Key Results

Compared with care in other ambulatory settings, subspecialty ILD clinic care was associated with the following adjusted outcomes:

|Outcome|Adjusted effect|95% confidence interval|
|:---|:---|:---|
|Chest CT imaging around the diagnostic period|**OR 2.51**|2.07–3.05|
|Antifibrotic therapy|**OR 1.54**|1.32–1.80|
|Lung biopsy|**OR 0.47**|0.32–0.68|
|All-cause hospitalization|**HR 0.46**|0.42–0.51|
|Emergency department visit|**HR 0.68**|0.59–0.78|
|Death|**HR 0.57**|0.45–0.72|

All six associations were statistically significant at p < 0.001.

The diagnostic and treatment pattern is consistent with more guideline-concordant evaluation and management: more cross-sectional imaging and antifibrotic treatment alongside fewer lung biopsies. The time-to-event results also show lower hazards of hospitalization, emergency department visits, and death among patients seen in the ILD clinic.

## What This Means for Health Systems

1. **Specialty expertise needs to extend beyond the clinic walls.** Referral optimization, telemedicine, and clinical decision support could help bring ILD expertise to patients who are not reached by existing pathways.
2. **Access should be measured directly.** Counting patients in a specialty clinic cannot reveal who remains outside it. Health systems need to examine the full ILD population across ambulatory settings.
3. **Unspecified diagnoses are a useful signal.** Their higher frequency in non-specialty settings points to an opportunity for earlier diagnostic review and more targeted specialist input.
4. **Quality and equity are linked here.** The differences in diagnostic evaluation, treatment, utilization, and mortality appeared alongside demographic and socioeconomic differences in who reached each setting.

## Caveats & Limitations

* **Associations are not causation:** Patients were not randomized to care settings. These results do not establish that ILD clinic placement alone caused the improved outcomes.
* **Residual confounding remains possible:** Referral patterns, disease severity, access barriers, and other unmeasured characteristics may differ between the groups.
* **Single-system evidence:** The study was conducted at one large academic medical center, so the magnitude of the associations may differ elsewhere.
* **Interventions still need evaluation:** Referral optimization, telemedicine, and decision support are plausible responses, but their effects on access and outcomes should be tested rather than assumed.

## Take-Home Message

Within one academic health system, subspecialty ILD clinic care was associated with more guideline-concordant evaluation and treatment, less acute-care utilization, and better survival. Patients in other ambulatory settings were disproportionately from groups already facing structural barriers to care.

The challenge for health systems is not only to build ILD expertise, but to make that expertise accessible and measurable across every care pathway.
